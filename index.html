Banks are financial services companies. Each of these eight Globally Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs) varies in structure, but their operations consist of a common set of functions: investment banking, financial services for consumers and small businesses, commercial banking, transaction processing, and asset management. For the everyday person these show up as ordinary interactions with products such as checking or savings accounts, credit cards, or brokerage accounts for investing. Frequency Banks

Total Revenue

Total revenue represents the bank's reported operating revenue which is bifurcated into net interest income and noninterest revenue. As you'll see from the trends below, J.P. Morgan consistently grosses the largest amount of revenue while State Street and Bank of New York Mellon are consistently the two smallest. This is primarily due to the nature of each bank's operations: J.P. Morgan is a full-service bank whose operations are tied to the founding of the U.S. economy, whereas State Street and Bank of New York Mellon are a primarily a custodial bank who serves as an intermediary for more complex financial transactions.

Commentary

Top-line results this quarter highlight the structural divergence between pure-play investment banks and commercial lending institutions. JPMorgan Chase continues to demonstrate a commanding scale advantage at $57.35B in total revenue, of which $25.51B is net interest income — roughly the entire quarterly revenue of several peers. Morgan Stanley, by contrast, draws far more of its total from noninterest components, including $6.72B of trading revenue against just $2.78B of net interest income.

Note on comparability. Component definitions vary significantly by filer. Trading revenue in particular splits across disparate XBRL concepts depending on how management defines trading desks versus market-making portfolios, and some filers stop tagging a concept entirely when they change presentation — which is why certain trading series end earlier than others on the chart above. Analyzing the trend shape within a single institution is therefore a more reliable exercise than benchmarking levels across banks.

Net Income

Net income is the bank’s “bottom line” — what remains available to distribute to shareholders or reinvest after operating expenses, taxes, and funding costs. Diluted earnings per share divides that figure by the total of current and potential shares that would exist if all options and obligations were exercised.

Commentary

Profitability converts to shareholder returns at very different rates. JPMorgan reports the largest absolute net income at $21.16B, while Goldman Sachs posts the highest diluted EPS at $20.98 on net income of $6.63B — roughly a third of JPMorgan’s earnings spread across a far smaller share count.

Note. Diluted EPS reflects share repurchase programs as well as organic earnings growth. When a bank retires its own stock the denominator shrinks, so EPS can rise even in a quarter when net income does not — which is why the two charts above do not always move together.

Consumer Credit Quality

The provision for credit losses is a forward-looking estimate of losses each bank expects to incur in the future. It is booked as a charge against current earnings, so it reduces net income in the quarter it is taken — before any actual loss is realized.

Commentary

The provision is one of management’s most significant financial statement estimates, and its size tracks business mix more than it tracks any single quarter’s outlook. JPMorgan and Citigroup carry large consumer lending books and book provisions of roughly $2.5B each. Goldman Sachs and BNY Mellon, whose franchises center on institutional asset management, custody, and investment banking rather than consumer credit, recorded $0.10B and $0.01B respectively.

Direction matters as much as level. Both JPMorgan and Citigroup came in below their prior-quarter provisions, so the current build reflects the scale of the loan portfolio rather than a deteriorating credit outlook. A provision rising materially above its recent range is the signal that management expects conditions to worsen.
